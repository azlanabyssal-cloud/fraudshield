The Real Numbers

Every figure on this page carries its source and a status: read at the source, reported by named outlets, or calculated here. A number without one does not get printed.

Cyber Crime Cases Registered in India

Source: National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB), Crime in India 2021–2024 (the 2024 report came out in May 2026)

📌 Registered cases rose 92% between 2021 and 2024. In 2024 the NCRB recorded 1,01,928 cases, and 72.6% of them (73,987) were fraud.
📌 Registered cases are a small part of what people report. In 2025, 28.15 lakh cyber fraud cases were recorded and 55,484 FIRs were registered, about 2%. The two numbers come from different registers, so read the gap as a guide, not an exact rate.

Where the Money Went, 2025

Share of the rupees reported lost, by type of fraud. Source: Ministry of Home Affairs reply in Parliament, as reported by ThePrint

  • Investment fraud — 76% of the money (35% of cases)
    Fake trading apps, Ponzi schemes and crypto scams.
  • Digital arrest — 9% of the money (6% of cases)
    Fake police or agency calls that keep you on a video call.
  • Sextortion — 4% of the money (19% of cases)
    Many cases, little money per case.
  • Everything else — 11% of the money
    Calculated: 100% minus the three types above.
📌 Investment fraud was 35% of cases but 76% of the money. The scams that cost the most are not the most common.

The Two Highest States, 2024

Source: NCRB, Crime in India 2024 (cyber crime cases). The full state table could not be read at the source, so only these two are shown.

📌 Telangana recorded 27,230 cases and Karnataka 21,003. Counts also depend on how readily each state registers cases, so a high count is not by itself a measure of risk.

Reported Losses, ₹ Crore

Source: Ministry of Home Affairs replies in Parliament, as reported (2023–2025)

📌 Reported losses were ₹22,845 Cr in 2024 and ₹22,495 Cr in 2025, a change of -1.5%, while cases rose 24%. The average reported loss per case fell from ₹1,00,728 to ₹79,911. These figures cannot say why: more small cases, faster freezing and changes in how people report could all play a part.

FORENSIC TIMELINE · 2000–2026

How Digital Fraud Evolved in India

Four distinct eras, each shaped by a leap in technology — and each eventually met by a leap in the state's response.

2000–2010

Incubation & Anonymity

Fraud lived in public cyber cafés — Nigerian "419" advance-fee emails and rudimentary stalking. Low sophistication, high friction: India's banking sector wasn't yet digitised enough for fast money movement.

2011–2018

The Jamtara Vishing Boom

Cheap smartphones decentralised fraud from urban hackers to rural syndicates. The "Jamtara model" — named after a Jharkhand district — turned voice phishing into an industry: fake bank executives harvesting OTPs and CVVs by phone.

2019–2023

Real-Time Payments Exploitation

NCRB cases: 52,974 (2021) → 86,420 (2023)

UPI's frictionless rails became the getaway car. Fake "receive money" QR codes, remote-access apps disguised as customer support, and predatory loan apps harvesting entire contact lists for blackmail — some cases ending in tragedy.

2024–2026

Industrialisation & Synthetic Deception

1,01,928 cases in 2024 · reported losses up 206% on 2023

AI-driven deepfake voice and video, multi-day psychological "digital arrests," and automated mule-account networks that launder stolen funds into crypto within minutes. The most sophisticated era yet — met by the most sophisticated countermeasures yet.

THE STATE FIGHTS BACK

India's Real-Time Defence Systems

Technology this precise didn't exist five years ago. Here's what's actually intercepting fraud right now.

🧭

Pratibimb

Maps fraud infrastructure

I4C's GIS-based tracking module maps the physical location of active fraud numbers, giving police direct visibility into "fraud factories."

🤖

MuleHunter.AI

Live in 26 banks

RBI's machine-learning tool for detecting mule accounts, rolled out to banks and being scaled up (PIB, March 2026). It looks for patterns such as rapid fund-out right after a credit.

📋

Suspect Registry

32.08 lakh mule accounts shared

A shared registry of suspect identifiers run by I4C with banks. By 30 June 2026 it had received 30.48 lakh suspect identifiers from banks, shared 32.08 lakh Layer 1 mule accounts with participants, and declined transactions worth ₹25,698 Cr.

📵

SIM & Device Blocking

15.75 lakh SIMs · 5.77 lakh IMEIs

Blocked on police reports, cumulative to 30 June 2026, according to the Ministry of Home Affairs reply in Parliament, cutting off the physical tools of the trade.

💰 New rules, not yet in force: on 24 June 2026 the RBI issued final directions that widen customer protection beyond unauthorised transactions and add compensation for small-value fraud: up to 85% of the loss, capped at ₹25,000, once in a lifetime, if reported to 1930 and the bank within 5 days. They apply to transactions from 1 January 2027.

🔓 Frozen money: since April 2026 the Money Restoration Module on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal lets victims apply to get frozen money back, using the 14-digit complaint number. A police report is enough up to ₹50,000 in a single account; above that an FIR is compulsory. Only money that was frozen before it was withdrawn can be returned, and recovery is not guaranteed.

Sources: Ministry of Home Affairs replies in Parliament (as reported) · I4C · RBI and PIB releases · Money Restoration Module reports (The420.in, India TV).
